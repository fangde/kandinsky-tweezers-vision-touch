Abstract

Dexterous robotic hands are typically used to grasp a tool while its Cartesian motion is generated by the robot arm. In many human precision-manipulation tasks, however, the wrist remains nearly stationary and small tool motions are produced primarily through coordinated finger motion. Examples include manipulating tweezers, forceps, needles, pens, and other precision instruments.

This paper presents a model-based visual–tactile control framework for precision in-hand manipulation of tweezers using a multi-finger dexterous hand with a fixed wrist. An analytical hand–tool kinematic model maps finger joint velocities to tool motion through finger–tool contact constraints. Visual feedback directly measures the Cartesian pose of the tweezer tip and compensates for accumulated modeling and contact errors. Tactile or force feedback independently regulates internal grasp forces to maintain stable finger–tool contact.

The resulting control architecture separates three functions: analytical kinematics determine coordinated finger motion, visual servoing corrects tool-tip positioning error, and tactile feedback maintains grasp stability. The target application is millimeter-scale manipulation within a local workspace, such as moving the tweezer tip by ±5 mm along arbitrary Cartesian directions while maintaining tool orientation. The proposed framework provides a simple and interpretable alternative to end-to-end learning for high-precision dexterous tool manipulation.

02

I. Introduction

Dexterous robot hands possess significantly more internal degrees of freedom than conventional grippers. Nevertheless, in most robotic tool-use systems, these degrees of freedom are mainly used to establish a stable grasp. Once the tool has been grasped, Cartesian manipulation is usually performed by the arm.

This differs from many forms of human precision manipulation.

When writing with a pen, manipulating tweezers, inserting a needle, or performing delicate assembly, the hand can generate several millimeters of accurate tool-tip movement while the wrist remains approximately stationary.

This motivates a different decomposition of robotic manipulation:

$$\text{robot arm} \rightarrow \text{coarse positioning},$$
$$\text{dexterous hand} \rightarrow \text{fine positioning}.$$

The objective of this work is therefore to investigate fixed-wrist dexterous tool manipulation, where all local tool motion is generated by finger articulation.

The specific task considered in this paper is:

A dexterous robotic hand holds a pair of tweezers. The robot wrist remains fixed, while coordinated finger motions move the tweezer tip within a local Cartesian workspace of approximately ±5 mm.

The objective is not merely to manipulate the fingers, but to directly control the Cartesian state of the tool tip.

Classical multifinger manipulation research established analytical relationships between finger kinematics, contact constraints, object motion, and grasp forces. Cole, Hauser, and Sastry, for example, formulated the kinematics and control of multifingered manipulation with rolling contact and friction constraints.

Direct robotic manipulation of tweezers has also been studied. Senoo et al. analyzed finger–tweezer contact and combined it with high-speed visual servoing to grasp small objects with tweezers in both 2-D and 3-D experiments.

Visual servoing has subsequently been demonstrated for controlling the Cartesian position of an object held by a multifingered hand, using visual information to reject disturbances and close the object-level control loop.

More recent work has investigated differentiable rolling-contact models and trajectory optimization for multi-finger manipulation, as well as tactile force regulation for improving robustness under changing contacts.

Rather than relying exclusively on an accurate analytical model or replacing the model entirely with reinforcement learning, we propose a hybrid approach:

$$\boxed{ \text{Analytical Kinematics} + \text{Visual Error Correction} + \text{Tactile Force Regulation} }$$

The analytical model provides fast and interpretable motion coordination. Vision compensates for inaccuracies in the hand–tool model, while tactile sensing regulates grasp forces and detects contact degradation.

II. System Definition

Consider a dexterous hand with \(n\) actuated finger joints.

Let

$$q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$$

represent the hand joint configuration.

The wrist is assumed to be fixed during fine manipulation:

$$\dot q_{\mathrm{arm}}=0.$$

The tweezers are modeled using two components:

  1. the six-dimensional rigid-body pose of the tool;
  2. one internal degree of freedom describing opening and closing.

The rigid-body pose is

$$T_o= \begin{bmatrix} R_o&p_o\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} \in SE(3),$$

and its spatial velocity is

$$V_o= \begin{bmatrix} v_o\\ \omega_o \end{bmatrix}.$$

Let

$$\alpha$$

denote the opening state of the tweezers.

The generalized tool velocity is therefore

$$\nu= \begin{bmatrix} V_o\\ \dot\alpha \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^{7}.$$
04

III. Finger–Tool Kinematic Model

Suppose that \(m\) fingers maintain contact with the tool.

For finger \(i\),

$$p_i=f_i(q_i),$$

and the contact-point velocity generated by the finger is

$$\dot p_i=J_i(q_i)\dot q_i.$$

Stacking all finger contacts gives

$$v_c=J_h(q)\dot q,$$

where

$$J_h= \operatorname{diag}(J_1,\ldots,J_m).$$

Thus,

$$\boxed{ v_c=J_h(q)\dot q. }$$
(1)

Now let \(r_i(\alpha)\) denote contact \(i\) expressed in the tool coordinate frame.

Its world position is

$$p_{c_i}=p_o+R_or_i(\alpha).$$

Differentiation gives

$$\dot p_{c_i} = v_o + \omega_o\times R_or_i + R_o \frac{\partial r_i}{\partial\alpha} \dot\alpha.$$

This can be written as

$$\dot p_{c_i}=A_i(T_o,\alpha)\nu.$$

Stacking all contacts,

$$v_c=A(T_o,\alpha)\nu.$$

Assuming that the contacts remain locally sticking,

$$\boxed{ J_h(q)\dot q=A(T_o,\alpha)\nu. }$$
(2)

Equation (2) forms the central analytical model.

It expresses the relationship

$$\text{finger joints} \rightarrow \text{contact motion} \rightarrow \text{tool motion}.$$
05

IV. Tool-Tip Task Model

The actual manipulation objective is expressed at the tweezer tip rather than at the finger contacts.

Let

$$p_t$$

be the midpoint of the two tweezer tips.

The desired Cartesian task can be represented as

$$x= \begin{bmatrix} p_t\\ \phi_o \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^{6},$$

where \(p_t\) represents tip position and \(\phi_o\) represents tool orientation.

If opening is also controlled,

$$x= \begin{bmatrix} p_t\\ \phi_o\\ d \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^{7},$$

where

$$d=\|p_t^+-p_t^-\|$$

is tip separation.

Tool-tip velocity is related to the generalized tool velocity through

$$\boxed{ \dot x=H(T_o,\alpha)\nu. }$$
(3)

Equations (2) and (3) therefore provide a complete analytical chain:

$$\boxed{ \dot q \rightarrow J_h \rightarrow v_c \rightarrow A \rightarrow \nu \rightarrow H \rightarrow \dot x_{tip}. }$$

This can be interpreted as a hand–tool task Jacobian.

Locally,

$$\dot x_{tip} = J_{\mathrm{task}}\dot q.$$

Unlike a conventional arm Jacobian, \(J_{\mathrm{task}}\) includes both the robot hand and the grasped tool.

06

V. Cartesian Tool-Tip Controller

Let the desired tool-tip pose be

$$x_d.$$

The measured pose is

$$\hat x.$$

The Cartesian error is

$$e_x=x_d-\hat x.$$

A resolved-rate controller generates

$$v_x^* = \dot x_d+K_pe_x.$$

For point-to-point positioning,

$$\dot x_d=0,$$

and therefore

$$\boxed{ v_x^*=K_p(x_d-\hat x). }$$
(4)

The desired finger velocities can then be obtained through constrained optimization:

$$\begin{aligned} \min_{\dot q,\nu}\quad& \|H\nu-v_x^*\|_{W_x}^2 + \lambda_q\|\dot q\|^2 + \lambda_p\|q-q_{\mathrm{nom}}\|^2 \\ \text{s.t.}\quad& J_h(q)\dot q=A(T_o,\alpha)\nu, \\ &q_{\min} \le q+\dot q\Delta t \le q_{\max}, \\ &\dot q_{\min} \le \dot q \le \dot q_{\max}. \end{aligned}$$
(5)

This QP formulation provides coordinated finger motion while respecting joint limits and maintaining a preferred grasp configuration.

07

VI. Visual Tool-Tip Feedback

Pure analytical control assumes that

$$J_h,\quad A,\quad H$$

accurately describe the real system.

This assumption is difficult to satisfy for a dexterous hand holding tweezers.

Errors arise from:

  • imperfect finger calibration,
  • soft fingertip deformation,
  • changing contact locations,
  • tendon transmission error,
  • small amounts of slip,
  • compliance of the tweezers,
  • uncertainty in the initial grasp.

For this reason, the proposed controller closes the feedback loop directly around the quantity that matters:

$$\boxed{x_{tip}}.$$

An external camera, stereo camera, optical tracker, or marker-based system estimates

$$\hat x_{tip}.$$

The visual system does not replace the analytical controller.

Instead, it provides the error term in Eq. (4):

$$e_x=x_d-\hat x_{tip}.$$

Consequently, even if the analytical model predicts an imperfect joint motion, the next control cycle observes the remaining Cartesian error and corrects it.

The architecture therefore becomes

$$x_d \rightarrow e_x \rightarrow \text{Cartesian controller} \rightarrow \text{model-based QP} \rightarrow \dot q \rightarrow \text{hand/tool}$$

with

$$\hat x_{tip}$$

fed back from vision.

Multifinger visual-servoing experiments have previously shown that object-level visual feedback can be combined with kinematic hand models for closed-loop Cartesian manipulation.

For precision tweezers manipulation this is particularly useful because measuring the tool tip directly avoids requiring perfect estimation of every finger–tool contact state.

08

VII. Tactile and Grasp-Force Control

Visual feedback controls where the tweezers go.

It does not guarantee that the fingers maintain a stable grasp.

Let the vector of finger contact forces be

$$f= [f_1^T,\ldots,f_m^T]^T.$$

The net wrench acting on the tool is

$$w=Gf,$$

where \(G\) is the grasp matrix.

Finger joint torques satisfy

$$\boxed{ \tau=J_h^Tf. }$$
(6)

Contact forces can be decomposed into

$$f=f_{\mathrm{task}}+f_{\mathrm{int}},$$

where internal forces satisfy

$$\boxed{ Gf_{\mathrm{int}}=0. }$$
(7)

These forces increase grasp stability without producing net tool motion.

Therefore a tactile controller can regulate

$$f_{\mathrm{int}}\rightarrow f_{\mathrm{int}}^d.$$

For example,

$$\tau_f = J_h^T K_f (f_d-\hat f).$$
(8)

The complete joint command can conceptually be decomposed as

$$\boxed{ \tau = \tau_{\mathrm{motion}} + \tau_{\mathrm{grasp}}. }$$

The motion controller generates the coordinated movement required for tool-tip tracking, while the grasp controller maintains sufficient normal force to prevent slip.

Recent tactile in-hand manipulation studies have shown that adding contact-force regulation and compliance to optimization-based manipulation can improve robustness relative to open-loop trajectory execution.

VIII. Complete Visual–Tactile Control Architecture

The proposed system therefore contains three nested functions.

A. Tool-Space Control

Visual sensing measures

$$x_{tip}.$$

The Cartesian error is

$$e_x=x_d-x_{tip}.$$

The desired tool velocity is

$$v_x^*=K_pe_x.$$

B. Model-Based Hand Coordination

The analytical model solves

$$J_h\dot q=A\nu$$

and

$$H\nu\approx v_x^*$$

through the QP in Eq. (5).

This produces coordinated finger motion.

C. Contact-Force Regulation

Tactile sensing measures

$$f_i.$$

Internal grasp forces are regulated toward

$$f_d$$

without intentionally changing tool motion.

The overall architecture is therefore

$$\boxed{ \begin{array}{ccc} &&x_d\\ &&\downarrow\\ \text{Vision} &\rightarrow& \text{Tool-Space Error Controller} \\ &&\downarrow\\ && \text{Analytical Hand–Tool Model} \\ &&\downarrow\\ && \text{QP Inverse Kinematics} \\ &&\downarrow\\ \text{Tactile Feedback} &\rightarrow& \text{Finger Control} \\ &&\downarrow\\ && \text{Tweezers} \end{array}}$$

The three sensing/modeling roles are intentionally separated:

$$\boxed{ \text{Model: how should the fingers move?} }$$
$$\boxed{ \text{Vision: did the tool tip actually arrive?} }$$
$$\boxed{ \text{Tactile: is the tool still securely grasped?} }$$

IX. Simplified Two-Dimensional Example

Consider two planar 2-DOF fingers holding a rigid tool.

Let

$$q= [q_{11},q_{12},q_{21},q_{22}]^T.$$

The planar tool state is

$$\xi= [x,y,\theta]^T.$$

Assume the two contacts are positioned symmetrically at

$$r_1=(-a,0)^T, \qquad r_2=(a,0)^T.$$

For a horizontal tool,

$$A= \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\ 0&1&-a\\ 1&0&0\\ 0&1&a \end{bmatrix}.$$

The contact constraint is

$$J_h(q)\dot q=A\dot\xi.$$
(9)

Suppose the desired operation is to translate the tweezer tip 5 mm to the right while maintaining orientation:

$$\Delta\xi_d= \begin{bmatrix} 5\\ 0\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \mathrm{mm}.$$

Then

$$A\Delta\xi_d = \begin{bmatrix} 5\\ 0\\ 5\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \mathrm{mm}.$$

Therefore both contacts should move approximately 5 mm to the right.

The model computes an initial joint displacement:

$$\Delta q = J_h^\dagger A\Delta\xi_d.$$

Suppose model error causes the real tool to move only

$$4.6~\mathrm{mm}.$$

The camera observes

$$x_{\mathrm{measured}}=4.6~\mathrm{mm}.$$

The residual error becomes

$$e_x=5.0-4.6=0.4~\mathrm{mm}.$$

The next iteration commands

$$v_x^*=K_p(0.4).$$

The analytical model therefore does not need to produce exactly 5 mm in a single open-loop command.

Instead,

$$5 \rightarrow 4.6 \rightarrow 4.93 \rightarrow 4.99 \rightarrow 5.00~\mathrm{mm}.$$

This is the principal benefit of combining analytical kinematics with visual feedback.

At the same time, the tactile loop maintains approximately constant normal contact force:

$$F_{n,1}\approx F_{n,1}^d,$$
$$F_{n,2}\approx F_{n,2}^d.$$

Thus positioning accuracy and grasp stability are controlled independently.

X. Online Jacobian Adaptation

An optional extension is to allow the analytical task Jacobian to adapt to systematic model errors.

Recent work on purely in-hand pen writing demonstrated that the complete hand–object task Jacobian can be estimated directly online, without an analytical contact model. The physical system achieved a reported mean in-plane tracking error of approximately 0.6 mm while generating pen motion through finger articulation alone.

This suggests an intermediate strategy between pure analytical modeling and end-to-end learning.

Let

$$J_a$$

be the analytical task Jacobian.

Introduce a correction

$$\Delta J.$$

Then

$$\boxed{ J_{\mathrm{effective}} = J_a+\Delta J. }$$
(10)

The analytical model provides the initial structure, while observed tool motion updates \(\Delta J\).

For example,

$$\Delta x_k = J_k\Delta q_k$$

can be used to recursively update \(J_k\).

This approach preserves physical structure while compensating for contact uncertainty.

It may be especially useful when the same hand repeatedly manipulates the same class of tweezers.

XI. Experimental Evaluation

A proposed experimental platform consists of:

  • a multi-DOF dexterous hand,
  • a fixed robot wrist,
  • joint encoders,
  • fingertip tactile or force sensors,
  • a conventional pair of tweezers,
  • an external optical tracking or vision system.

The arm first positions the hand close to the manipulation region.

The arm is then locked.

The hand performs all subsequent fine motion.

A. Point-to-Point Motion

The first benchmark consists of six Cartesian targets:

$$(+5,0,0),$$
$$(-5,0,0),$$
$$(0,+5,0),$$
$$(0,-5,0),$$
$$(0,0,+5),$$
$$(0,0,-5)$$

in millimeters.

Orientation should remain constant.

A representative requirement is

$$|\Delta\theta|<0.5^\circ.$$

B. Trajectory Tracking

The tool tip subsequently tracks:

  • a line,
  • a 5-mm-radius circle,
  • a square,
  • a figure-eight,
  • a three-dimensional helix.

C. Tweezer Operation

The final experiments include simultaneous pose and opening control:

$$x= [p_x,p_y,p_z,\phi_x,\phi_y,\phi_z,d].$$

The hand moves toward a small target and then reduces

$$d$$

to grasp it.

This evaluates whether tool translation and functional actuation can be performed simultaneously.

XII. Ablation Study

A particularly useful experiment is to compare four controllers.

Controller A: Analytical model only

$$J_{\mathrm{analytic}}$$

with open-loop execution.

Controller B: Analytical model + vision

$$J_{\mathrm{analytic}} + \text{visual servo}.$$

Controller C: Analytical model + vision + tactile control

$$J_{\mathrm{analytic}} + \text{visual servo} + \text{force control}.$$

Controller D: Online task Jacobian

$$J_{\mathrm{online}}$$

with visual feedback.

The comparison should measure

$$RMSE_{tip},$$

maximum Cartesian error,

$$e_R,$$

repeatability,

contact slip,

force variation,

and task success rate.

This experiment would directly answer an important scientific question:

How much physical modeling is actually necessary for millimeter-scale intrinsic dexterous tool manipulation?

14

XIII. Discussion

The proposed architecture differs from conventional tool manipulation in an important way.

A conventional system typically uses

$$\text{hand} \rightarrow \text{grasp},$$
$$\text{arm} \rightarrow \text{tool motion}.$$

The proposed architecture instead uses

$$\text{arm} \rightarrow \text{coarse positioning},$$
$$\boxed{ \text{hand} \rightarrow \text{local Cartesian manipulation}. }$$

The dexterous hand therefore becomes a second manipulator embedded at the end of the robot arm.

Its workspace may only be several millimeters or centimeters, but this workspace can be exploited for precision.

The concept resembles a coarse/fine actuator system:

$$\text{large workspace + moderate precision}$$

from the arm, combined with

$$\text{small workspace + high precision}$$

from the fingers.

This distinction is particularly relevant for surgical manipulation, laboratory automation, electronics assembly, and other tasks involving small instruments.

The proposed approach also avoids requiring the analytical hand–tool model to be perfectly accurate.

Instead:

$$\text{physics}$$

provides structure,

$$\text{vision}$$

provides accuracy,

and

$$\text{touch}$$

provides contact robustness.

Trajectory-optimization methods with detailed rolling and geometric constraints could subsequently extend the framework to larger in-hand motions where contact locations must move substantially.

15

XIV. Conclusion

This paper proposes a model-based visual–tactile framework for precision dexterous manipulation of tweezers using finger motion alone.

The analytical core is

$$\boxed{ J_h(q)\dot q=A(T_o,\alpha)\nu, }$$

combined with

$$\boxed{ \dot x_{tip}=H(T_o,\alpha)\nu. }$$

This model converts desired tool-tip motion into coordinated finger motion.

Visual feedback closes the control loop directly around the tweezer tip:

$$\boxed{ v_{tip}^* = K_p(x_d-x_{tip}). }$$

Tactile feedback independently regulates internal grasp forces:

$$\boxed{ Gf_{\mathrm{int}}=0. }$$

The resulting architecture therefore combines

$$\boxed{ \text{model-based motion planning} + \text{visual precision} + \text{tactile grasp stability}. }$$

For a target manipulation range of approximately ±5 mm, this formulation avoids the complexity of full contact-implicit planning while remaining substantially more robust than purely open-loop analytical control.

More broadly, the approach suggests a hierarchical manipulation principle in which the robot arm performs gross positioning while the dexterous hand acts as a local, high-precision manipulator around the tool tip.
